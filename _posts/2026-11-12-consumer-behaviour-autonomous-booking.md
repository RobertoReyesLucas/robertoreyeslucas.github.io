In part one, I quoted a survey in which 86% of consumers said they preferred a chatbot to a form or had no preference. In part two, I argued that this preference tends to break down at one specific moment, when the bot asks for personal details. For this post, I wanted to test both ideas with my own data.

The paradox appeared straight away. Most of the people I surveyed (83%) had used a chatbot on a company website in the past year, and 71% agreed that a chatbot would get them information faster than a contact form. Yet when I asked which channel they would choose to book an appointment or ask for a quote, only 3% picked the chatbot.

## Method

I ran an exploratory online survey of 35 consumers living in Spain and Ireland, during the first week of November 2026. Respondents ranged in age from 18 to over 55, and most questions used a five-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

To test the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), I adapted items from Davis (1989) to measure perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use and intention to use a chatbot to book or request a quote. The remaining questions came from the framework in part two: disclosure, trust, privacy, what data people would share and when they would rather deal with a person. Throughout the results, "agree" combines the two highest points of the scale.

## Results: Where Trust Breaks

The clearest result is the gap between booking and sharing details. Just over half of respondents (51%) would book an appointment through a chatbot, but only 31% would leave it their name, email or phone number. About three times as many people rated booking higher than sharing their details as the other way round, and the difference was statistically significant. This is the trust gap from part two, measured in my own data.

Willingness to share also falls as the data becomes more sensitive. Most respondents were happy to give their name or email address (61% each) or their preferred dates (58%), while far fewer would share their phone number (36%) or their budget (30%). This matches what I saw in hotel campaigns, where asking for a budget or a phone number too early made users drop off. It supports Step 3 of my framework: give value first and leave the sensitive questions until the end.

Disclosure turned out to be less of a problem than I expected. A clear majority (86%) wanted to be told at the start that they were talking to an AI, and nobody disagreed. Only 29% said that knowing it was a bot would make them less likely to continue. This nuances Luo et al. (2019), who found that disclosing a chatbot before the conversation cut purchases by more than 79.7%. Their study measured real sales and mine measures stated intentions, so the two are not directly comparable, but in my survey the demand for transparency was far stronger than any rejection of it.

A human safety net also makes the chatbot more acceptable. Eight in ten (80%) would be more willing to use one if they knew they could switch to a person at any time. As one respondent put it: *"The possibility of being able to talk to a real person at any time, if I had any problem."*

The moments when people want that person are the delicate or expensive ones: negotiating a price (68%), making a complaint (59%) and confirming an expensive booking or purchase (47%). Only 6% would want a person to book a simple appointment. Complaints deserve particular care, as Crolic et al. (2022) found that angry customers respond worse to humanlike chatbots, which is exactly why Step 4 of my framework hands these conversations over.

## Does TAM Hold? And an Unexpected Finding on Budgets

Broadly, yes. The people who saw the chatbot as useful and easy to use were also the most willing to use it, as Davis (1989) predicted. Ease of use also made the chatbot seem more useful, so a smooth conversation pays off twice. Even so, these two factors explained only about a quarter of the difference between the people who would use the chatbot and those who would not.

Trust turned out to matter just as much. In my view, this is the most useful finding for marketers: a chatbot can be useful and easy to use and still lose the lead if people do not trust it. Pizzi et al. (2023) reached a similar conclusion, finding that when consumers see a chatbot as competent and warm, they are less sceptical of it and more willing to share personal information. Rejection also seemed to come from a lack of perceived value more than from the AI label itself, as the people most put off by talking to a bot were those who saw the least use in it.

One result went against what I expected. In part two, I suggested that people might find it easier to tell a bot their budget, based on Lucas et al. (2014), who found that people disclosed more to a virtual interviewer because they feared being judged less. In my survey, only 21% would feel more comfortable telling a chatbot their budget than a salesperson, and 41% disagreed. My interpretation is that the fear is of a different kind. In a clinical interview, people worry about being judged; with money, they worry about what the company will do with the information, for example adjusting the price they are offered.

## What This Means for Marketers

These results translate into four practical rules for anyone running lead capture with a chatbot:

1. ***Use the bot for simple tasks.*** Answering questions, checking availability and booking simple appointments are where users accept automation most easily.

2. ***Ask for the phone number and budget last.*** Collect name, email and dates first, and only ask for sensitive data once the user has received something useful, such as matching listings or a live price.

3. ***Say it is an AI in the first message.*** Users expect it, and the EU AI Act now requires it (see part two).

4. ***Keep the human handoff one click away.*** Show the option from the start, and trigger it automatically for price negotiations, complaints and high-value bookings.

Privacy remains a concern. Half of respondents (51%) were worried about how companies use the details they share with a chatbot, and some simply asked for reassurance: *"If it assured me that my data wouldn't be saved."* I will return to this in part five.

### Limitations

This was a small, exploratory convenience sample, with a mix of respondents from Spain and Ireland concentrated in two age groups, so the results cannot be generalised. The scales were also short, with two questions per factor. This partly explains why the answers on intention to use were less consistent: one question asked about booking and the other about sharing details, and respondents treated them very differently. That difference is the trust gap itself.

The biggest limitation is that a survey measures what people say they would do, which is not always what they do in practice. My own results show how wide that gap can be: 71% said a chatbot would get them information faster than a form, yet only 3% would choose one to book. Real campaign data, such as conversion and drop-off rates, would be needed to confirm these findings.

Consumers are clear that they want a person available for the difficult moments, so the next question is whether businesses can actually provide one. In part four, I interview a marketing practitioner about running AI funnels in real client campaigns, including whether that human handoff is affordable and whether real conversion data tells the same story as my survey.

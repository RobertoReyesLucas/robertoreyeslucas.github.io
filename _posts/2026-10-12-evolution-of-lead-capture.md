Despite major advances in programmatic advertising and AI-driven audience targeting, the destination for many digital campaigns remains remarkably basic: the static landing page. Marketing teams spend thousands of euros driving high-quality traffic to their websites, only for prospects to fill out a form and wait hours or even days for a reply.

This delay creates two distinct drop-off points. First, users often abandon forms before submitting them, partly because typing into empty fields feels tedious. Second, those who do submit often lose interest while waiting for a response, causing the momentum of the initial ad click to fade. The problem is also growing: a 2021 survey of 503 B2B professionals by Drift and Heinz Marketing reported a 27% rise in frustration with traditional forms (Drift Insider, 2021, cited in Mirosavljević and Milovanović, 2022).

To reduce this friction, marketers can use virtual assistants and chatbots, which help reduce uncertainty during the pre-transaction phase by answering questions and guiding users in real time (Hoyer et al., 2020).

To address these drop-off points, modern digital strategies are moving towards conversational funnels. Rather than capturing an email address and leaving it in a CRM queue, systems such as AI-driven chatbots and automated booking tools engage prospects immediately, making it easier for them to decide while their interest is highest.

In theory, this should lower cost per acquisition by keeping prospective buyers engaged when they are most likely to convert, rather than leaving them waiting for a follow-up email.

However, user acceptance is more complex than simple preference surveys suggest.

A 2019 survey of US consumers showed that only 14% preferred traditional forms, while the remaining 86% either preferred a chatbot or had no specific preference (Figure 1). Yet stating a preference in a survey is not the same as trusting an automated system with personal details during a real purchase. Even so, in sectors with high enquiry volumes, conversational funnels can significantly reduce friction. In my experience with real estate campaigns and at Paraty Tech, a hotel marketing agency managing thousands of hotel websites, users frequently abandon long, static contact pages. A conversation can keep them engaged when their intent is strongest and can even close sales for some types of business where no sensitive information is needed.

## From Scripted Bots to Conversational AI

Lead capture has evolved in stages, and each stage tried to fix the weakness of the one before. For a long time, the static form was the standard: the user typed in their details and waited for someone to get back to them. Live chat was the first serious attempt to close that gap, since a real person could answer questions while the prospect was still on the page. The drawback was that it depended on staff being available, which limited it to office hours and made it expensive to scale. Rule-based chatbots then made it possible to answer at any hour. Mirosavljević and Milovanović (2022) identify this one-to-one, real-time communication, available 24 hours a day, as a prominent feature of conversational agents.

The latest shift works because the technology has advanced significantly. The industry is moving away from simple automated scripts to contextually aware artificial intelligence. Early chatbots merely followed strict rules and often annoyed users. Today’s conversational systems process natural language, determine user intent, and adapt the conversation in real time.

For marketing agencies and business owners, the campaign no longer stops when the user clicks the ad. The AI takes over the middle of the funnel. For example, in real estate lead generation, instead of making a prospect wait for an agent to email them back, the AI immediately asks about their budget, preferred location, and timeline. It qualifies the lead on the spot and can even secure a viewing appointment directly in the calendar.

## Inside an Autonomous Funnel

In this post, I use the term autonomous funnel to describe a lead management system that runs without human input until a lead is ready for a salesperson. The chatbot is only the visible part. Behind it, the system carries out four tasks that would traditionally be split between a form, a CRM and a sales team:

1. Responding. The prospect receives an answer within seconds, at any hour, while the ad is still fresh in their mind.
2. Qualifying. The system asks the questions a sales agent would normally ask first, such as budget, dates, location or specific needs.
3. Booking or selling. For simple purchases, it can complete the transaction. For more complex ones, it autonomously schedules a call or a viewing.
4. Handing over. The qualified lead, together with the full conversation, is passed to the CRM or directly to the right person.

Each of these tasks already existed in some form. The difference is that they now happen within a single conversation, which removes the waiting time between them, and that waiting time is precisely where the traditional funnel loses prospects. This also clarifies how the two terms in this post relate: the conversational funnel is the part the user interacts with, while the autonomous funnel is the system as a whole.

That said, deploying these AI systems does not guarantee a conversion. According to Drift’s research, it was found that around a third of US consumers had used chatbots but, based on that experience, decided to use them less or not at all (Drift Insider, 2021, cited in Mirosavljević and Milovanović, 2022). These figures should be read with some caution. The studies cited in this post were carried out between 2019 and 2021, before generative AI tools such as ChatGPT, launched in November 2022, reached a mass audience. The chatbots those consumers had in mind were far more limited than the ones available today, so attitudes have likely moved on since, and the data in part three will help show how far. Introducing bots into the sales process can make some consumers uncomfortable or hesitant to share their contact details. In part two, I will break down the psychology of consumer trust and analyse why some users still reject automated chats.

The rest of this series then moves from theory to evidence. Part three presents original data on how consumers behave in autonomous booking and lead capture, and part four brings in the views of marketing practitioners who use these tools in real campaigns.

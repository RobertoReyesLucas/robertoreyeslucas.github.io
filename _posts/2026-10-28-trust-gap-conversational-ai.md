In part one, I described a paradox. In a 2019 survey, 86% of US consumers said they preferred a chatbot to a form or had no preference. Yet around a third of US consumers had used chatbots and, based on that experience, decided to use them less or not at all.

Generative AI has not closed that gap. A Gartner survey of 5,728 customers, carried out in December 2023, found that 64% would prefer companies not to use AI in customer service (Gartner, 2024).

I call this distance between what people say and what they do the *trust gap*. In my experience with real estate campaigns, it appears at a precise moment: prospects chat happily about areas and property types, then go quiet when the bot asks for a phone number or a budget. In this post, I use three ideas from consumer psychology to explain why, and end with a five-step framework that marketers can apply straight away.

## Why We Treat Chatbots Like People, and Punish Them When They Fail

Back in the 1990s, Nass, Steuer and Tauber (1994) showed that people apply social rules to computers, even when they know they are dealing with a machine. This became known as the *Computers Are Social Actors* (CASA) paradigm. In practice, this means users judge a chatbot as they would judge a salesperson, for example on whether it seems competent and honest.

Designers, me included, often lean into this. Giving a chatbot a name and an informal, human-like way of speaking increases social presence and the emotional connection users feel with the company (Araujo, 2018). The problem is that a human-like bot creates expectations, and it then has to live up to them:

- ***Inflated expectations.*** The more human a bot seems, the more users expect from it, so a frustrated customer judges its mistakes more harshly.

- ***Unresolved errors.*** A bot that admits it has not understood and asks again keeps the user on board; one that confidently answers the wrong question loses them.

- ***The uncanny valley.*** A bot that tries too hard to look human, for example with a lifelike avatar, can feel unsettling rather than friendly.

In my view, marketers should therefore make sure the bot is good at its job before giving it a personality.

## Who is Speaking? Source Credibility in the Chat Window

Source credibility theory holds that a message is more persuasive when its source seems expert and trustworthy. In a chat window, however, the source is ambiguous: is it the bot, the software provider or the brand behind it?

Luo et al. (2019) show why this matters. In a field experiment with more than 6,200 customers, chatbots that did not reveal their identity sold as well as proficient human staff. When the bot disclosed that it was a machine before the conversation, purchase rates fell by more than 79.7%, because customers saw it as less knowledgeable and less empathetic. The drop was smaller when the bot revealed its identity at the end of the conversation, and among customers who had used AI before.

Hiding the bot is no longer an option in Europe, though. Since 2 August 2026, Article 50 of the EU AI Act has required that users are told they are interacting with an AI system, at the latest at the first interaction (European Union, 2024). Marketers now have to decide how to disclose it, because disclosing it is mandatory.

I don’t think disclosure has to mean losing the sale, as long as the bot is helpful from the first message. My personal experience suggests why: on a hotel's website, guests see the chat as part of the hotel, so a helpful bot makes the hotel look good and a clumsy one makes it look bad.

## The Privacy Calculus: When a Bot Beats a Human

According to the privacy calculus, every time users fill in a lead form they weigh the risk of sharing their data against their trust in the company and their interest in what they will get back, and trust and interest can outweigh that risk (Dinev and Hart, 2006).

A bot can sometimes feel safer than a person. In a clinical interview study, people disclosed more when they believed they were talking to a computer rather than a person, because they feared being judged less (Lucas et al., 2014).

Applied to lead capture, this suggests a division of roles:

- **The bot** handles questions that feel personal or open to judgement, such as budget or flexible dates.

- **A person** handles high-stakes or emotional moments, such as negotiating a price, resolving a complaint or confirming an expensive booking.

If trust depends on context, the marketer's job is to design that context.

## Closing the Trust Gap: A Five-Step Framework for Marketers

Based on these three ideas, I propose five steps, each linked to one of the four tasks of the autonomous funnel from part one.

1. ***Disclose early, and lead with what the bot can do (Responding).*** Open with one plain line stating that it is an AI assistant, what it can do and that a person is available. This sets realistic expectations and reassures customers that they can talk to a real person at any moment. For example: *"Hi, I'm the hotel's AI assistant. I can check prices for your dates in seconds, answer questions about our facilities and pass you to our reservations team at any time."*

2. ***Calibrate humanness (Responding).*** Use a friendly tone and fast, relevant replies rather than a lifelike avatar. If the user seems frustrated, switch to a plain, efficient style or offer a person.

3. ***Give value before asking for data (Qualifying).*** Order the questions from low to high sensitivity, show results first and ask for contact details last. In real estate, for instance: area and property type, then two or three matching listings, then timeline and budget, and only then a name and phone number to book a viewing.

4. ***Plan the human handoff before you need it (Booking or selling / Handing over).*** Hand over immediately when the user asks for a person, after two failed answers in a row, when frustration appears or when a deal is high-value. The agent should see the full conversation, so the user never has to repeat themselves.

5. ***Measure the trust gap, not just conversions.*** Track drop-off at each question, the handoff rate and post-chat satisfaction. Then A/B test one variable at a time, such as the disclosure wording or the order of the questions.

Most of these steps do not require extra budget, as they mainly involve changing how the conversation is designed.

## From Theory to Data

To sum up, whether users trust a lead capture chatbot depends on the expectations it creates, on who they think they are talking to and on how much they feel they are risking.

These findings come with limits. Most of the studies cited here are experiments from other sectors, and several were carried out before generative AI reached a mass audience. In part three, I move from theory to evidence with my own consumer survey on autonomous booking and lead capture.
